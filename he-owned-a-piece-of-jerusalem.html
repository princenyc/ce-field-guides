He Owned a Piece of Jerusalem. Then a Billionaire's Son Did.

CE Field Guides · Published August 19, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a Hall of Fame speech and ends with a stock filing that shows Stoudemire's name isn't on it anymore.

On August 15, 2026, Amar'e Stoudemire became the first Israeli citizen and the first Jewish player inducted into the Naismith Basketball Hall of Fame in more than fifty years. The speech covered twenty years of secret Torah study and a spiritual homecoming to Jerusalem. It didn't cover what happened to the ownership stake he bought in an Israeli basketball club back in 2013, or the two real antisemitism controversies his conversion survived along the way.

What Happened

In 2013, Stoudemire joined a four-investor group, led by Israeli-American entrepreneur Ori Allon, that bought a controlling stake in Hapoel Jerusalem. His personal share settled at roughly 5%. In 2016 he signed to play for the club as a retired NBA All-Star, and his contract reportedly required him to sell those shares back to Allon. He won a title with Hapoel in 2017 and got Israeli citizenship in 2019. Then in January 2020 he signed with Maccabi Tel Aviv, Hapoel's crosstown rival, and reporting at the time confirmed he still held his 5% stake despite the 2016 sale requirement. A club source called it "absurd." Later that year he completed an Orthodox conversion through a beth din, taking the Hebrew name Yehoshafat Ben Avraham.

2022 and 2023 were messier than the induction coverage suggests. Stoudemire left a Brooklyn Nets assistant coaching job over Shabbat conflicts, then publicly defended the Black Hebrew Israelite movement during the Kyrie Irving and Kanye West antisemitism controversies, arguing Irving's apology was only owed for "the little portion" of the film he'd promoted that was "hurtful to the Jewish people." In March 2023, on a Hebrew Israelite Clubhouse show with about 8,000 listeners, he agreed on-air that European Jews are "converts," a claim rooted in antisemitic conspiracy theory, and floated Jesus as the Messiah. He walked it back within 48 hours. In that same window, Matan Adelson, son of the late casino magnate Sheldon Adelson and his wife Miriam, bought 90% of Hapoel Jerusalem for $20 million, with the club's fan association holding the remaining 10%. Two years later: the International Jewish Sports Hall of Fame. This year: Naismith.

The Ownership Question

Ninety plus ten is a hundred. There's no reported slice left in that math for a minority holder from the original 2013 group. Ori Allon, the president who was supposed to receive Stoudemire's shares in 2016, had already exited the club himself, moving on in 2024 to buy Italy's Virtus Bologna. The most defensible read: Stoudemire's stake was sold off somewhere in the ownership churn between 2020 and 2023, folded into the same transition that moved the club from Allon's founding group to Adelson family control. Current biographical write-ups that still describe him as holding "a significant share" of Hapoel are working from stale information. It did not end with a press release or a statement from Stoudemire. It ended in a 2023 ownership filing showing 100% of the club split between two parties, and neither of them is him.

The bigger fact sitting under that one is who bought it. The Adelson family is among the largest Republican donor operations in American politics and one of the most consequential pro-Israel funding networks in the country, built on Sheldon Adelson's casino fortune and decades of direct support for Israeli political causes and aligned U.S. candidates. Miriam Adelson separately agreed to buy a majority stake in the NBA's Dallas Mavericks in a deal reported around $3.5 billion. The same family now holds a controlling position in an Israeli basketball club and is moving into American major-league ownership at the same time. Stoudemire, the Black American convert who helped build Hapoel's ownership story in the first place, appears to have been priced out of the club entirely along the way.

It ended in a 2023 ownership filing showing 100% of the club split between two parties, and neither of them is him.

Outlier, Not Pattern

Two separate stories get flattened into one whenever Black celebrities and Judaism come up. A physically present community of several thousand African Americans built a claim to Israeli identity on ancestry starting in the late 1960s, and spent decades refusing conversion on principle. See the glossary below for that history. Separately, celebrities including Kendrick Lamar, Kodak Black, Nick Cannon, and Kyrie Irving have referenced Hebrew Israelite identity in interviews, lyrics, or posts, without pursuing formal conversion, citizenship, or ownership.

Stoudemire fits neither lane cleanly. Equity ownership first, in 2013, years before any public religious statement. Formal Orthodox conversion second, in 2020, through the exact mechanism the older community rejected for decades. Citizenship third, in 2019, ahead of the conversion. No other Black American athlete or entertainer has completed that specific sequence, and current reporting shows no one following him into it. But before any of that, he spent years in the aesthetic-identification lane himself, at one point telling a reporter he was "spiritually and culturally Jewish" while, by his own later account, still attending a Hebrew Israelite congregation built around Jesus worship, and posting an Instagram caption reading "I'm not Jew-ish." A Jewish blogger publicly disputed media claims that he was Jewish at all during that stretch.

None of the 2022 or 2023 controversy stopped the institutional embrace that followed. That sequencing is the actual finding. Orthodox Jewish institutions extended recognition to Stoudemire not despite a clean religious record, but after two real, documented episodes of language the Jewish community itself flagged as antisemitic. The forgiveness was real. It is also forgiveness a community-scale claim to Jewish identity has never been extended.

The Reception Gap

Orthodox and Haredi communities have embraced Stoudemire warmly, controversies included. One Haredi columnist described being captivated watching him study religious texts and visit a rabbinical court in Bnei Brak. Jewish sports media groups him with Dolph Schayes and Sue Bird as part of a lineage of Jewish basketball icons.

Set that next to how Israel treats Ethiopian Jews, a population roughly fifty times the size of the older Hebrew Israelite community, who arrived through government-organized airlifts rather than individual celebrity conversion. After four decades in the country, Ethiopian Israelis still report acute discrimination. Poverty runs at 39%, against 14% for the general Jewish population. The Chief Rabbinate still does not fully recognize Ethiopian Jews and their children as Jewish, the same credentialing question Stoudemire cleared individually through his own beth din. Protests over police violence against Ethiopian Israelis have recurred for over a decade, and this year's demonstrations targeted the roughly 10,000 relatives still stranded because immigration programs remain on hold.

The gap isn't evidence that Orthodox Israel has gotten less racially closed. It's evidence that voluntary, individual, high-status conversion by a famous American gets a different reception than inherited Black or Ethiopian Jewish identity does at scale. A single convert who clears the Rabbinate's gate on his own terms is legible and non-threatening. A community of thousands claiming inherited status threatens the Rabbinate's authority to decide who counts as Jewish in the first place. That's the mechanism, and it holds regardless of whether Israel's broader racial politics are improving.

The Game Being Played

Pew's most recent numbers put favorable views of Israel among Black Protestants at 33%, among the lowest of any American subgroup measured, well below white evangelicals or Jewish Americans. Gallup separately found 65% of Democrats now sympathize more with Palestinians than Israelis, concentrated in the same demographic coalition Black American political opinion runs through.

Stoudemire has been publicly on the other side of that shift since October 7, 2023. The day after the attack, he posted: "I woke up this morning with some disturbing news out of Israel that Hamas [are] kidnapping children, putting them in cages, killing women, killing the elderly. That's some coward s**t... And for all y'all Black Lives Matter [supporters] who ain't saying nothing..." He named the exact political coalition and called it out directly. A Black American public figure with genuine standing in Orthodox Israel, taking a maximally pro-Israel public position, at the precise moment Black American opinion is moving furthest away from Israel of any major U.S. demographic. His Hall of Fame week put a dignified, mainstream media spotlight on that position at the worst possible moment for it to land quietly. Whether that makes him a bridge figure, a genuine outlier content to stay one, or eventually a target from within his own community isn't answered by anything in current reporting.

What's Still Unconfirmed

No source directly reports the sale of Stoudemire's Hapoel shares. The Adelson 90/10 math makes his exit near-certain by mid-2023, but the how and when are reconstructed, not documented. Whether the Jewish institutional embrace reflects genuine willingness to extend belonging to a Black convert specifically, or whether any high-profile convert who walked back antisemitic comments in 48 hours would get the same pass, is a live question with no answer in current reporting. A search for Black press or Black political and religious commentary on this specific induction turned up nothing as of publication. Every piece of coverage found originated from Jewish media or straight sports outlets covering it as a basketball story.

What to Watch

Black American polling on Israel over the next two quarters, specifically whether the Black Protestant subgroup keeps diverging from national averages. Any public reaction to Stoudemire's induction from Black political or religious figures, given none has surfaced yet. Any reporting that directly confirms how the Hapoel stake ended, and whether Matan Adelson's dual ownership of an Israeli club and, through his mother, a stake in the Dallas Mavericks draws NBA governance scrutiny given the family's donor profile.

Glossary: The Dimona Hebrew Israelites. A separate community from anything Stoudemire is part of, and the one most coverage wrongly folds into his story. Founded by Chicago-born Ben Ammi Ben-Israel (born Ben Carter) after a 1966 vision that he said told him Israel was the promised land for Black Americans descended from the ancient Israelites, the group led an exodus of American followers, first to Liberia, then to Israel's Negev desert, arriving in the late 1960s and through the 1970s. The core distinction: this community refused conversion. Israeli law allows citizenship for anyone who formally converts to Judaism. They rejected that path on principle, arguing they were already Israelites by descent. Israel didn't recognize the claim for decades, leaving the community without legal status and children unregistered at birth. The standoff eased gradually: permanent residency around 2003, the first member's full citizenship in 2009, more than forty years after arrival. Today an estimated 3,000 to 5,000 members live in Israel, concentrated in Dimona, Arad, and Mitzpe Ramon. Stoudemire's path runs the opposite direction: he used the conversion mechanism this community spent decades refusing, and cleared citizenship in six years, not forty.
