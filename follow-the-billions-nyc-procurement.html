Follow the Billions: The Black Business Guide to NYC Procurement

CE Field Guides · Published August 7, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a minority-contracting statistic and ends with two city offices publishing incompatible numbers for the same year.

New York City spends $46 billion a year buying things. Goods, services, construction, the machinery that keeps 8.5 million people fed, housed, and moving. It is one of the largest purchasing operations on Earth, run by an elected government, funded by taxpayers, and open in principle to any certified business in the five boroughs.

In FY25, Black and Hispanic-owned firms captured a share of that spending too small to move a decimal point.

That is the story most people will read. It is true. It is also not the interesting part.

Two Governments, Two Scoreboards

Ask the Mayor's office how the city did on minority contracting in FY25 and you get a press release. Record year. $6.9 billion in M/WBE contracts. A 36.4% utilization rate, the highest in the program's history, six months ahead of schedule. Confetti language.

Ask the Comptroller's office the same question and you get an audit. $2.4 billion. Five percent. Of $46 billion in citywide procurement, minority and women-owned firms took home one dollar in twenty. Only 2,478 of the city's 11,382 certified M/WBE firms won any work at all. Sixty-five percent of the contracts that did go to M/WBEs were registered after the work had already started, which means the vendor floated the city's cash flow before the city acknowledged the contract existed.

Same fiscal year. Same city government. Two numbers that cannot both be the headline, and both offices published theirs as if the other didn't exist.

This is not a lie. Nobody is caught fabricating data. The Mayor's 36.4% is real, it is just measuring a much smaller pool, the roughly $6 billion in contracts specifically covered by Local Law 1. The Comptroller's 5% is also real, and it is measuring the entire $46 billion the city spends. One office picked the denominator that makes the city look transformed. The other picked the denominator that makes the city look unchanged. Both denominators are legitimate. That is the game.

Both offices are doing their jobs. Neither has to reconcile its number with the other's.

The Mechanism, Not the Mission

The M/WBE program exists to correct a documented history of exclusion. Local Law 1, passed in 2013 and expanded by Local Law 174, sets utilization goals by industry. It is real policy with real legal teeth. It is also, every December and January, a press cycle. Whoever occupies City Hall gets to announce a record. Whoever occupies the Comptroller's office, an independently elected watchdog with every incentive to find the gap, gets to announce a crisis.

Both offices are doing their jobs. The mechanism that keeps this stable, year after year, three different mayors running, is that neither office has to reconcile its number with the other's. There is no requirement that the "record year" headline and the "meager share" headline agree. The city just produces both, on schedule, and lets whichever one fits your priors find you.

That is the incentive structure. Not fraud. A permission structure for two true, incompatible stories to run every year without friction.

Where the Money Actually Leaks

The Comptroller's office found something more specific than a percentage problem, buried in a companion report on the city's Master Agreements. One IT contract, active since 2022, worth $276 million and covering more than 70 manufacturers, had subcontract documentation filed for only ten of those entities. All ten were subsidiaries of Cisco Systems.

That is not an accounting quirk. That is a contract structured so that M/WBE participation can be claimed at the top of a master agreement while the actual dollars, the actual work, flow somewhere the city's own tracking systems cannot see. Mayoral agencies, citywide, have approved subcontractor records against just 5.83% of the prime contracts that are supposed to carry them. The city cannot currently tell you where most of its own subcontracting money goes. That is not a Black business problem specifically. It is a structural blind spot that Black-owned firms, disproportionately positioned as subcontractors rather than primes, absorb hardest.

What to Actually Watch

Skip the annual percentage fight. It will repeat every January and it will not resolve, because the two offices are not trying to answer the same question. Watch these instead:

The M/WBE Small Purchase Method. Direct, non-competitive purchases up to $1.5 million. No bid war, no bonding marathon. Usage is up 214% since the 2022 reforms, to $347.5 million in FY25. This is the actual front door. Everything else on this list is context. This is the leverage point.

Subcontract registration in PASSPort. Currently 5.83%. If this number moves, meaningfully, the city has started closing the gap the Comptroller keeps finding. If it doesn't move, the "record year" press releases are measuring a number that has nothing to do with where the money lands.

Local Law 77. Passed in 2025. It forces the Comptroller to publish this audit every year, on a fixed schedule, going forward. That is new. It means the dueling-scoreboard dynamic described above is no longer an occasional story. It is now a permanent annual fixture, and the gap between the two numbers, tracked year over year, is itself the signal worth watching.

Retroactive registration rate. 65% of M/WBE contracts in FY25 were registered after work began, against 66% of all citywide contracts registered within 30 days of their start. That inversion, M/WBE firms specifically waiting longer to get paid than the citywide average, is a cash-flow tax that never shows up in a utilization percentage.

What This Means If You're Building Something

If you run a certified or certifiable business, the Small Purchase Method is the highest-percentage path into city work available right now. Sub-$1.5 million, no competitive bid, growing fastest of any award category. Start there before chasing a Local Law 1 prime contract that will take longer to close and longer to pay.

If you're bidding as a sub on a large Master Agreement, get the subcontract terms in writing and confirm the prime is actually filing them in PASSPort. The Cisco example shows what happens when they don't. Your work can be invisible to the city's own accounting even while it's fully performed.

If you're reading this as a citizen, not a vendor, stop trusting either headline in isolation. When the Mayor's office says "record," ask what it's a record of. When the Comptroller says "meager," ask meager against what total. The two offices are not lying to you. They are each telling you the truth about a different city.
