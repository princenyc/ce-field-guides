Reparations Isn't Just an American Argument Anymore

CE Field Guides · Published August 31, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a UN document and ends with a question about who gets to decide whether the past still owes anyone anything.

On August 31, 2026, a United Nations treaty body published a document telling 182 governments that slavery being legal at the time is no longer a valid defense against reparations claims. The document is General Recommendation No. 40, issued by the Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination, the panel that monitors the treaty banning racial discrimination. It carries no enforcement power on its own. Whether it matters depends on who picks it up next.

How This Actually Works

CERD is not "the UN." It is the Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination, a panel of 18 independent experts. Its job is monitoring one treaty: the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (ICERD), signed in 1965, ratified by 182 states including the US, UK, France, and Portugal.

A General Recommendation is not a ruling. It is not a resolution. It is an interpretation. Think of it as the treaty body's authoritative reading of what the treaty already requires, issued to guide states, courts, and litigators on how to apply it. CERD has issued 40 of these since the treaty took effect.

Non-binding does not mean irrelevant. It means CERD cannot enforce this on its own. What it can do is sit on a shelf until a court, an attorney general, or a legislator decides to cite it. The document itself is not the leverage. The leverage is what someone else chooses to do with it.

That is the frame for everything below.

What Happened

On August 25, 2026, CERD adopted General Recommendation No. 40, on reparatory justice for the harms and continuing consequences of colonialism, the trafficking in enslaved Africans, and racialized chattel slavery. It published the document August 31.

The recommendation states that states parties "must implement comprehensive reparatory measures for people of African descent, covering all aspects of remedies." It lists monetary, non-monetary, and structural measures: restitution, compensation, rehabilitation, truth commissions, archive access, memorialization, education, and institutional reform.

The scope is wider than the transatlantic trade alone. Paragraph 6 states the focus on chattel slavery "does not exclude comparable systems, including those involving southern Africa and routes across the Sahara and the Indian Ocean, including Mauritius."

The Argument Underneath the Argument

Governments have relied on one defense for decades: slavery was legal when it happened, under the laws that existed then. In international law this is called the intertemporality principle. An act only breaches an obligation if the state was bound by that obligation at the time the act occurred.

GR-40 does not dismiss this doctrine. It engages it directly, then narrows it. The Committee distinguishes a completed historical act from a continuing breach, and states plainly that the continuing effects of a completed act are not automatically a continuing breach on their own (para. 28-29). But it goes further. Paragraph 30 states that "even if certain practices were not explicitly prohibited by treaty at the time, general principles of law, customary international law and the later crystallization of jus cogens norms render such defence insufficient."

Jus cogens are peremptory norms of international law. They apply regardless of what any individual treaty said at the time. The prohibition on racial discrimination sits in this category, per the Committee (para. 24). That is the mechanism by which "it was legal then" gets defeated without CERD needing to argue the transatlantic trade was illegal under the treaties of its own era.

The Committee also cites a competing legal and philosophical tradition. Paragraph 9 references the Manden Charter of 1235, the oral constitution of the Mali Empire, and the principle that "a crime does not rot." No time limit exists for redressing a wrong. Time does not diminish a violation. It carries the wrong forward as a continuing present reality until there is reparation. This is not decoration. It is CERD offering an alternative framework to the Western statute-of-limitations logic that has blocked reparations claims in court for decades.

"Denying effective reparatory justice is itself a distinct form of racial discrimination and violates article 2 (2) of the Convention."

That reframes inaction. Refusing to act is not neutral under this reading. It is itself a violation.

Why It Matters

Reparations debates have stalled for decades on one question: why should a government today pay for something that was legal centuries ago. GR-40 is an attempt to make that question obsolete by replacing it with a different one. Are today's racial inequalities the continuing consequence of a system states have an obligation to remedy now, regardless of what the law said then.

That is a venue shift, not just an argument. Reparations has lived mostly in legislatures, universities, activist organizations, and commissions. A treaty-body interpretation hands lawyers a new pathway: historical harm, to continuing discrimination, to treaty obligation, to legal or policy remedy. Whether courts accept that chain remains unproven. But opponents now have to argue against it specifically, rather than simply outlasting the political conversation.

What Would Prove This Wrong

GR-40 has no enforcement mechanism. It becomes consequential only if someone uses it. Two things to watch over the next 12 months.

Whether any court cites GR-40 in a ruling or judicial review.

Whether any government body treats it as an input rather than ignoring it. HR 40 hearings in the US. A state attorney general's opinion. A foreign ministry response.

The US case needs its own caveat here. When the Senate ratified ICERD in 1994, it attached a declaration that the treaty is non-self-executing. That means individuals cannot sue directly under ICERD in US courts without implementing legislation, and Congress has never passed any. GR-40 does not change this. A US court can still cite it as persuasive authority, the way courts cite law review articles or foreign rulings, but no one gets a US cause of action out of this document. For the domestic reparations conversation specifically, GR-40 is a rhetorical and diplomatic asset, not a new legal tool. Anyone treating it as one is overselling it.

If neither of the two triggers above happens within a year, the "present-day legal obligation" framing was aspirational. The venue shift will still be real. The results will not be.

There is already a live counterexample working against the thesis. In 2023, France's Court of Cassation denied reparations to plaintiffs from Martinique on standing and statute-of-limitations grounds (Appeal No. 22-13.457). That ruling predates GR-40, but it is exactly the kind of decision GR-40 is trying to preempt. If French courts, or any others, keep ruling this way after GR-40's publication, the legal theory is failing in practice regardless of how sound it is on paper. Watch this case and any successors to it specifically, not the broader legal debate in the abstract.

Who Else Is Watching

Reparations arguments do not stay inside a human rights frame once a rival state has a use for them. Russia has publicly backed African reparations claims against Western former colonial powers. Former President Dmitry Medvedev has argued those states have "neither legal nor moral grounds" to evade reparatory justice. Russia supported the March 2026 UN General Assembly resolution recognizing the transatlantic slave trade as a crime against humanity, a resolution the US and some European states opposed.

None of this makes the underlying legal argument in GR-40 wrong. But it means the document does not exist only inside a human rights conversation. It is also a tool available to any government looking to put a Western power on the defensive, and worth watching for who amplifies it and why, separate from whether its legal reasoning holds up.

What Compliance Actually Looks Like

Most of GR-40 stays abstract: remedies, obligations, doctrine. One existing example grounds it. The Netherlands issued a formal state apology for its role in slavery and established a €200 million fund covering education, research, commemoration, and community initiatives. That is the closest real-world precedent to what GR-40 is asking every state party to do. It is also small relative to the scale of harm GR-40 describes, and voluntary rather than compelled by any binding process. That gap between this one example and GR-40's full ask is the actual distance the document is trying to close.

What CE Is Actually Watching

CERD/C/USA/CO/10-12, the Committee's most recent concluding observations on the United States, cited repeatedly in GR-40's footnotes. This is the specific US review document, not a general assessment.

The Revised CARICOM Ten Point Plan for Reparatory Justice (2026), cited directly in GR-40's own footnotes as an existing regional framework the Committee points to as precedent.

HR 40, the US House bill to establish a commission studying reparations proposals, still in committee as of this writing.

Any court ruling, anywhere, that cites GR-40 by name.

The CE Question

Reparations debates have run for decades on a version of consent: does society agree this happened, does society agree it still matters. GR-40 tries to sidestep that question entirely. It asks a different one. Which institution already has the duty, and the authority, to repair this, whether or not the public has reached agreement.

What changes when the question stops being about persuasion and starts being about jurisdiction.

Glossary

ICERD — The International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination, a 1965 UN treaty with 182 state parties, including the US, UK, France, and Portugal.

CERD — The Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination, the 18-member panel of independent experts that monitors ICERD.

General Recommendation — A treaty body's interpretation of what a treaty already requires. Not binding law, but citable by courts and governments.

Intertemporality principle — The doctrine that an act only violates international law if it broke an obligation that existed at the time the act happened.

Jus cogens — Peremptory norms of international law that apply regardless of what any specific treaty said at the time. The prohibition on racial discrimination is treated as one of these.

Continuing breach — A legal category for a violation that persists over time, as distinct from a completed historical act whose effects merely continue.

Non-self-executing — A US treaty status meaning the treaty does not create rights individuals can sue under in US courts without separate implementing legislation from Congress. ICERD carries this status. Congress has never passed implementing legislation.

CARICOM — The Caribbean Community, a bloc of 15 Caribbean nations. Its Reparations Commission maintains a Ten Point Plan for Reparatory Justice, cited directly in GR-40 as an existing regional framework.
