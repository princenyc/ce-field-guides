Somebody Always Pays

CE Field Guides · Published September 12, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a Grand Slam semifinal and ends with an invoice nobody wrote down.

Ben Shelton and Frances Tiafoe met in a US Open semifinal. Two Black American men, one final spot. It was the first time that had happened in the Open Era. Whoever won would be the first Black American man in a US Open final since Arthur Ashe in 1972.

Shelton won in four sets. He plays Alexander Zverev Sunday.

The headline writes itself. Representation, history, a fifty-year gap closing on Arthur Ashe Stadium. That story is true and it is also the easy one. The harder question is what it took to keep two Black kids inside a sport that bills families six figures a year before either of them earns a dollar.

Two Kids, Two Different Subsidies

Frances Tiafoe's father fled a civil war in Sierra Leone and took a construction job building a tennis center in College Park, Maryland. When it was finished, he stayed on as head of maintenance and was given a spare office to sleep in. His sons grew up in that office. At five, a coach at the center waived their fees. At eight, Tiafoe got a full scholarship into the center's development program. A coach there personally sponsored his early tournament travel. By his teens, he had moved into USTA's own National Training Center in Boca Raton, a second layer of institutional support stacked on the first.

None of that ran through his family's bank account. It ran through a nonprofit built for a community his family had no prior claim to, then through the USTA's own player pipeline.

Ben Shelton's path looks nothing like it. His father, Bryan Shelton, reached No. 55 on the ATP tour, then spent over a decade as a Division I head coach, first at Georgia Tech, then at Florida. Ben's coaching was free because his coach lived in his house. He turned pro out of the University of Florida with a modest junior ranking, a wild card, and none of the debt to the international junior circuit most future pros grind through for years. In 2023, Bryan gave up a public university salary of roughly $225,000 a year to coach his son full time on tour, with no guaranteed income to replace it.

Two Black men, same semifinal, same headline. One got there through an institution built to catch kids with nothing. The other got there because his family already had, in the house, the exact expertise that institution exists to supply everyone else.

Worth naming without dwelling on it: Tiafoe's parents are immigrants who arrived in the 1990s. Shelton's father is African American, three decades inside American tennis before Ben ever picked up a racquet. Same weekend, same first-Black-American-man headline, two different starting points. One family found an institution. The other already had one, in the house.

The Older Test

Before either of them, there was another version of this story, and it looked like the exception that broke the pattern. Richard Williams taught Venus and Serena on public courts in Compton. No academy, no federation, just a father and a plan.

That lasted three years. In 1991 he moved the family to a private academy in Florida, where the coach trained both girls for free in exchange for fifteen percent of their future professional earnings, and covered the family's housing, food, and transportation to make it work.

One family found an institution. The other already had one, in the house.

Even the most famous self-made story in American tennis runs through someone else's money. The difference is only the shape of the deal. A nonprofit facility trades access for mission. A father trades free coaching for proximity. A for-profit academy trades free coaching for equity. Three families, three decades apart, and not one of them paid retail.

The Price Of Not Having One Of These

The traditional route, the one without a subsidy, has a number attached. Families on the international junior circuit report spending $7,500 to $45,000 a year on coaching alone, and up to $42,000 more on travel and entry fees. Industry estimates put the total cost of developing a world-class player north of $50,000 a year, sustained over a decade before a player earns anything back.

College tennis runs a fraction of that. Travel drops to $3,000 to $10,000 a year. Coaching is usually built into the scholarship.

Shelton had a mediocre junior ranking by top-five standards. He turned pro straight out of the NCAA anyway. Stop treating that as a consolation path. The international junior circuit costs $50,000 to $100,000 a year before a player earns a cent. College tennis costs a fraction of that, with coaching and travel absorbed by the scholarship. For a family without junior-circuit money, NCAA tennis isn't the backup plan. It's the plan. Steering a talented kid away from four-year college tennis toward the traditional junior grind may be steering them toward the more expensive, less proven route.

Who Else Is Built On This

The USTA Foundation runs the Frances Tiafoe Fund, which has raised over a million dollars since 2023 using his own story as the pitch. JTCC's own materials lead with $20 million-plus in alumni scholarships and a growing list of tour-level names. Rick Macci still trades on the Williams deal decades later, in interviews, in a Hollywood movie, in the fifteen percent he negotiated in 1991. Florida and Georgia Tech got a national championship and a recruiting story out of Bryan Shelton's decade on their sidelines.

Institutions built around subsidizing Black talent do not do it for free. They get fundraising material, alumni prestige, equity stakes, or recruiting wins. That is not a criticism. It is the mechanism. Nobody in this story is paying out of pure generosity, and the ones who paid early are the ones collecting now.

USTA itself sits inside its own version of this tension. It invests $25 to $30 million a year in player development, a small fraction of what the US Open alone brings in from broadcast rights, and it has an open, reported fight inside the organization over whether that money should concentrate on a handful of elite prospects or spread across mass participation. The organization's own stated goal, 35 million players by 2035, pulls against the elite pipeline it also claims to want. Which side wins that argument determines whether the next Tiafoe finds an institution waiting, or doesn't.

What To Watch

Whether USTA's coaching-diversity partnership with the American Tennis Association produces an actual headcount, not another press release. Whether the HBCU varsity coaching grant, currently capped at $3,000 per program, moves at all. Robin Montgomery, the other JTCC product on tour, whose career has stayed in the sport's middle tier rather than its top ten, proof that the subsidy gets a kid to the pros and nothing about it guarantees more than that.

What Would Break This

Every case in this piece ran through somebody else's money. A nonprofit facility, a parent's own professional career, a private academy's bet on future earnings. A Black American man or woman reaching a major final having come up through the full-price junior circuit, no subsidy at any stage, would be the case that breaks the pattern.

That case does not exist yet. If it shows up, this thesis gets rewritten. Until then, the question under every Black breakthrough in this sport is not whether the talent was real. It's who was already positioned to catch it.

Glossary

JTCC — a nonprofit tennis training center in College Park, Maryland, and the first site the USTA designated as a regional training center for its elite junior program.

NJTL — a nationwide network of tennis and after-school programs for kids from lower-income communities, started by Arthur Ashe in 1969.

ITF — the body that runs international junior and professional tennis tournaments outside the four majors, and keeps the world rankings players need to qualify for bigger events.

RTC — a regional training site the USTA designates to develop its top junior players. JTCC was the first.

Player Development — the USTA's own in-house program for coaching its most promising young players, separate from JTCC and the NJTL network.
