# The Rescue Model: How Black Archives End Up Owned By Someone Else's Money ## What happened In 1942, John H. Johnson founded Johnson Publishing Company in Chicago. Ebony and Jet became the record of Black American life that no white publication would run. Moneta Sleet Jr. photographed Coretta Scott King at her husband's funeral and won a Pulitzer for it. David Jackson photographed Mamie Till looking into her son's open casket, and that photograph helped ignite the civil rights movement. Johnson Publishing sold the magazines in 2016 but kept the photo archive. Three million negatives and slides. 983,000 prints. 166,000 contact sheets. 9,000 audio and video recordings. The most complete photographic record of 20th century Black life that exists. In 2019, the company filed for bankruptcy. The archive went to auction. A consortium bought it for $30 million: the Ford Foundation, the J. Paul Getty Trust, the MacArthur Foundation, the Mellon Foundation, and the Smithsonian Institution. In 2022, ownership transferred to the Getty Research Institute and the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture. Every institution involved called it a rescue. Historians called it a rescue. The Smithsonian's own secretary called it a gift to the country. No one is on record disputing any part of it. ## Why this exists Archives cost money that Black-founded institutions built during Jim Crow rarely had time to accumulate. Johnson Publishing ran on subscription and ad revenue for seventy years, not endowment income. When the money ran out, there was no Black-led foundation with $30 million sitting in reserve to buy the archive back. The buyers who could move that fast were the ones who already had the capital: legacy philanthropic institutions built on 20th century industrial and oil fortunes. This is not a plot. It is what happens when preservation requires capital and the capital sits somewhere else. ## Who gains The Getty Research Institute and the Smithsonian gain a world-class collection neither built. Both get to attach their names to the permanent record of Black America. The consortium foundations gain the credibility of having "saved" a national treasure, a reputational asset that outlasts the $30 million they spent. Getty's own representative said as much at the time: the acquisition would produce a series of publications carrying Getty's name. The archive itself gains resources. Getty and Smithsonian conservation budgets dwarf anything Johnson Publishing could have spent on digitization and climate-controlled storage. The images are more accessible now than they were the day before the sale. ## Who pays Johnson Publishing's founders and their heirs lose the asset entirely, along with any future revenue the archive generates through licensing, publication, or exhibition. The Black press as an institution loses its last major independent archive. There is no equivalent Black-owned counterpart to Getty Images left standing. Every future licensing decision, every framing choice about which images get published and which stay in storage, now runs through institutions Johnson Publishing never controlled. ## The blind spot The word "rescue" does the work here, and it's not wrong. The alternative to this sale was real: the archive could have been broken up, sold piecemeal, or lost to storage neglect. But "rescue" also forecloses the question of why no Black-led institution was positioned to be the rescuer. It reframes a structural gap in Black institutional capital as a happy ending rather than a symptom. ## The actual game This isn't a fight over interpretation. Nobody is contesting captions or curatorial framing. The game is capital velocity. Whoever can move $30 million fastest in a bankruptcy auction gets to be the permanent steward of record, regardless of who built the thing being stewarded. Financial distress is the mechanism. It doesn't require malice or a grab. It only requires one side having reserve capital and the other side not having it. The same mechanism played out at Fisk University, a historically Black college that spent nearly a decade in litigation before being forced to sell half its Stieglitz art collection to Alice Walton's Crystal Bridges Museum to stay solvent. Different content, same pressure: an under-capitalized Black institution holding a valuable asset it cannot afford to keep whole. ## Signals to watch Whether Getty and the Smithsonian publish work drawing directly on Johnson Publishing photographers' original captions and context, or whether the archive gets reframed through outside curatorial voices. Whether licensing revenue from the archive flows back to the Johnson family or Chicago's Black cultural institutions in any form. Whether other Black-founded archives currently under financial strain, community newspapers, historically Black college collections, independent Black museums, follow the same bankruptcy-to-consortium path in the next few years. ## What happens next Absent a change in who holds reserve capital, expect more of this, not less. Any Black-founded cultural institution running on operating revenue rather than endowment is a financial shock away from the same outcome. This would be wrong if a Black-led foundation with comparable capital emerges and starts winning these acquisitions instead, or if legislation changes how distressed nonprofit archives can be transferred. Neither currently exists. ## What the reader should do If you sit on a board, run a nonprofit, or advise family wealth connected to Black cultural institutions, endowment building matters more than program spending right now. An archive with no endowment is an archive one bankruptcy away from new ownership. Watch for the next distressed sale. It's coming from somewhere.
