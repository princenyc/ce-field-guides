The Equity Audit: How to Tell Whether NYC's Racial Plan Is Real

CE Field Guides · Published August 4, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a government press release and ends with a Charter clause that quietly answers the enforcement question nobody asked out loud.

New York City published a list of promises. Two hundred agency goals. Eight hundred strategies. Six hundred indicators, spread across 45 agencies, meant to expose where the city fails Black New Yorkers and track whether it stops.

The numbers behind the plan are ugly and confirmed. Black New Yorkers face a 65.6 percent True Cost of Living shortfall rate. White New Yorkers face 43.7 percent. In Manhattan the gap widens to 79.7 percent versus 32.9 percent. Median household net worth: $276,900 for white New Yorkers, $18,870 for Black New Yorkers. Almost fifteen times the gap. The city's own report notes that beats the national wealth gap of roughly nine times over.

Those figures come from the Urban Institute's ATTIS model, built for the city's Mayor's Office of Equity and Racial Justice. They are not spin. They are the baseline the plan says it will move.

Here is the question that matters. What happens when an agency misses its target.

The Power Underneath

The city's Racial Justice Commission wrote the Commission on Racial Equity into the City Charter in 2022. Section 3404 spells out what that body can do. It can propose community equity priorities. It can propose outcome indicators. Propose for possible inclusion in future plans.

Propose is the operative word. The Commission can publish a list. It cannot enforce one.

That is not a flaw hiding in the fine print. That is the design. A city can hit every indicator on a 600-item list while Black net worth, business ownership, and housing stability stay exactly where they are, because none of the 600 indicators carry a penalty for missing them.

Watch what agencies choose to measure closely. Program enrollment numbers move easily and look good in an annual report. Land ownership, procurement dollars actually paid out, and displacement rates move slowly and cost political capital to fix. A metrics system with no enforcement teeth rewards agencies for chasing the first kind of number and calling it progress.

The Faster Threat Isn't the Commission

Within hours of the April 6 release, the Trump DOJ's Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights, Harmeet Dhillon, called the plan "fishy" and said it "reeks of equal protection violations." That is one federal official's public position, not a filed complaint, but it points at something the plan's own accountability structure does not have: a fast-moving external actor with real enforcement power.

The irony sits right on the surface. The city built an advisory body to hold itself accountable and got no teeth. The federal government showed up with teeth aimed at killing the plan before an agency misses a single indicator.

Track this separately from the Commission's own timeline. A DOJ civil rights action moves on a different clock and answers to different incentives. If it lands, it could shape or stall the plan's implementation regardless of what the Final Plan says in May.

What to Watch

Four things determine whether this becomes real accountability or another report nobody reopens.

Does the Final Plan attach numbers and deadlines to the 600 indicators, or leave them open-ended. Past city equity efforts have flagged a lack of enforcement teeth as an unresolved problem. Nothing in the Charter fixes that on its own.

Does the DOJ turn its statement into an actual filing, and against what part of the plan.

Does the city's FY2027 budget fund the Office of Equity and the Commission at a level that survives a projected $5.4 billion deficit, or gets cut first.

Does procurement data, the actual dollars paid to Black-owned and women-owned businesses rather than certification counts, move at all. That number is harder to fake than an indicator on a list.

The plan's predecessor, the Taskforce on Racial Inclusion and Equity under the prior administration, ran for two years and produced grants, mental health expansions, and a savings program for kids. None of that shows up in a net worth number. Watch for whether this plan breaks that pattern or repeats it.

What This Is

Don't grade this plan by its language. Grade it by land, contracts, capital, and who stays housed. Check back in May when the Final Plan lands, and check the DOJ's next move before you check the Commission's.
