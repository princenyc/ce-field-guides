How a Data Center Gets Approved Before Anyone Notices

CE Field Guides · Published August 9, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a word missing from a zoning code and ends with a utility deciding what counts as a secret.

In Richland Parish, Louisiana, the utility building power infrastructure for Meta's data center is allowed to file the project's actual electricity demand as "Highly Sensitive Protected Material." That means the public version of the utility's own load filings, the document meant to tell ratepayers what's coming, doesn't include the number for the facility projected to draw roughly twice the power of the entire city of New Orleans.

That's not a leak in the process. It's a feature a regulator approved.

The Setup

Most people can't tell you what a data center is or does. That's not a knowledge gap that happened by accident. Few places require a public hearing before one gets built, and even fewer make the company disclose what it will actually cost the grid before the deal is signed. Two mechanisms do most of that work, and neither one is a data center problem specifically. They're a disclosure problem that data centers happen to expose.

The First Mechanism: The Zoning Gap

Most zoning codes were written before anyone thought to define "data center" as its own category. When the code is silent, a developer can often argue the project qualifies as a warehouse or light industrial use, which is usually permitted by right. By right means no hearing, no comment period, no vote. The community's first notice can be a construction crew.

The Second Mechanism: The Confidentiality Shield

Utility commissions routinely let companies mark commercially sensitive information as confidential in regulatory filings. That rule exists for ordinary reasons, protecting trade secrets and competitive pricing. Applied to a data center's projected electricity load, it does something else: it keeps the public from seeing the one number that determines whether their bill goes up. In Louisiana, a sitting member of the state's own utility commission has publicly objected to how far this has been stretched. The objection didn't change the filing.

By right means no hearing, no comment period, no vote. The community's first notice can be a construction crew.

The Game

This was never really about data centers. It's about who controls the timeline of disclosure. Whoever writes the zoning code, and whoever gets to decide what counts as sensitive, decides whether a community finds out before a decision is final or after. States are competing hard for this investment right now, and speed is the product they're selling. Louisiana created a fast-track review process in December 2025 that can approve a data center's power infrastructure in roughly seven to eight months instead of the standard twelve, as long as the company covers at least half the cost. Georgia and Virginia are running versions of the same trade under different names. Whichever version wins gets copied.

Who Gains, Who Pays

The company gets power fast, often at a rate the public never sees. The utility gets a guaranteed return on new infrastructure it gets to build regardless of who ultimately pays for it. Local officials get a jobs announcement, usually built on construction employment that disappears once the building is finished. Ratepayers carry the risk if the cost-recovery structure isn't built to protect them, and in most states, that structure is still being negotiated in real time, mostly without public input. Neighbors carry the rest: water drawn down, air permits for backup generators, noise, land value, often without a number attached to any of it before construction starts.

What People Are Missing

There used to be a federal tool built for exactly this problem. EPA's EJScreen mapped pollution burden against demographic data by census block, flagging communities already carrying a heavier environmental load than most. It was removed from EPA's website in February 2025. A lawsuit over the removal was dismissed in March 2026. It hasn't come back. A handful of states, New Jersey, Massachusetts, Minnesota, have their own version written into law. Most don't. Right now, in most of the country, there is no live public tool to check whether a proposed site sits in a neighborhood that's already carrying more than its share.

What to Watch

New utility commission dockets naming large loads or data centers specifically. That's where the real cost fight happens, and it's usually invisible until someone asks about it directly.

Local zoning amendments that quietly add or redefine an industrial use category. That's the moment the by-right door either closes or gets propped open for good.

Whether your state has, or is building, its own environmental justice review requirement. The federal version isn't coming back on its own.

What to Do With This

Don't file this under "data centers are bad." The technology isn't the story. A system built so a community can find out last, after the zoning is set and the load is filed as confidential, is the story. That system is still being written, state by state, right now.
