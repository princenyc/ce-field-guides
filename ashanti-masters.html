Ashanti's Masters: Who Holds What

CE Field Guides · Published October 30, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a claim to own her masters and ends with a contract nobody outside the room has read.

Ashanti has not shown that she owns her original masters. She has shown, in her own words, that she holds the right to make new ones.

Those are different assets.

By Irv Gotti's own account, two parties held the 2002 recordings. Universal owned half. He owned half. In 2022 he sold his half to an investment firm called Iconoclast. Asked on The Breakfast Club whether Gotti owns her masters, Ashanti said it was "a conversation for Universal."

Her plan is to compete with the old recordings instead of buying them back. She announced it in September 2021. The public record now shows an announcement and no album.

Confidence: high on the dispute and its dates. Low on who owns what today.

Who's In This

Ashanti released her debut in April 2002 on Murder Inc. and Def Jam. It opened at No. 1 with 503,000 copies, then a record for a woman's debut. She left Murder Inc. in 2009 and now releases through her own label, Written Entertainment.

Irv Gotti founded Murder Inc. He died February 5, 2025. In November 2021 he said he owns the masters and a sizable part of the publishing. His own later account to Billboard put his share of the masters at 50%.

Universal Music Group held the other half of the masters, by Gotti's account. In 2023 it released a 32-track deluxe edition of the debut on Republic Records.

Iconoclast is the investment firm that bought Gotti's half in 2022. Variety reported $100 million for the stake, plus $200 million in film and TV funding for Gotti.

Mark DeBarge and Etterlene Jordan wrote DeBarge's "Stay With Me." They are credited on "Foolish" because Ashanti's single is built on a sample of it. They show that the song layer has owners the masters dispute never mentions.

Why This Exists

The label business was a catalog business. The hit records were the lure.

Gotti said so plainly. Giving artists their masters is, in his words, "how I make my money." He argued that Ja Rule and Ashanti earn millions from live shows and he sees none of it. The recordings were the one thing he could sell.

Murder Inc. ran as an imprint of Def Jam. By Gotti's account, Universal held half the masters. When the deal came up for termination, he said Universal offered him $1 million. In 2022 he sold his half to Iconoclast.

Two clocks run on a catalog. The artist controls neither.

Clock one: the contract. Recording deals bar an artist from remaking old songs for a set period. The long-standing term is the later of five years after delivery or two to three years after the contract ends. Universal reportedly moved new deals to seven and five years in 2021. Ashanti's last major-label album came out in 2008. Under the old norm her restriction would have lapsed around 2013. (arithmetic on the norm. Her real terms are unknown.) Why she announced in 2021 is unexplained.

Clock two: the statute. U.S. law lets an author terminate a transfer of copyright 35 years after the grant, inside a five-year window, with notice served two to ten years ahead. Works made for hire are excluded. Labels argue sound recordings are works made for hire. For a 2002 grant the earliest effective date is about 2037, with notice from about 2027. (our arithmetic)

The test case is Salt-N-Pepa v. UMG. On January 8, 2026 a federal court in New York dismissed the duo's suit because they had not shown they owned the copyrights. The court declined to rule on work for hire but signaled the recordings likely qualified. The appeal is pending in the Second Circuit (No. 26-253). The defendant is the company Gotti says co-owned Ashanti's recordings.

No court has ruled that a 2002 recording can be taken back. The earliest date for Ashanti is a decade out. The re-record is the only lever in reach. (inference)

Who Gains, Who Pays

The money trail is partial. What exists is below.

Universal holds a stake by Gotti's account and released the 2023 deluxe edition. Its earnings from the catalog are unreported.

Iconoclast paid for Gotti's half. What it earns from Ashanti's tracks is unknown.

Gotti took cash in 2022. He said Ja Rule was the only artist who got a cut, a seven-figure check.

Ashanti signed her first deal at 14 and says she has held seven deals with seven majors. The cost of the re-record is undisclosed.

Artists on similar deals pay as a class, but no ownership data by race turned up. The closest figure is a BMG review of 33 acquired labels. It found lower recorded royalty rates for Black artists at four of them, by 1.1 to 3.4 percentage points. That measures royalty rates. It does not measure who owns masters.

The one cut nobody has reported is the artist's.

Does the Re-Record Work?

It worked once, for Taylor Swift. For Ashanti it is untested.

1989 (Taylor's Version) drew 368 million U.S. on-demand streams in its first chart week. Streams of the original fell 42.7% from the week before. Streams of the original Red ran 41% lower over the 18 weeks after its re-record. Luminate says the plan worked because the new versions became the definitive ones for her most devoted fans.

Swift built that audience around one artist's name over more than a decade. Whether a catalog audience built around a label's sound behaves the same way is unknown. No smaller-artist comparator turned up.

On July 13, 2022 Ashanti said her album would be out before year end. It has not appeared in any source we found through October 2026. The Season 2 trailer coverage still describes the re-record as in progress. This reflects public sources and streaming listings, not an audit of every catalog.

What People Are Missing

Four assumptions fail against the record.

"She owns her masters." That was the headline in September 2021. Nine months later she told Billboard what she held: the right to re-record and own the new masters. Outlets kept running the first version.

"Gotti owned them." He said so in November 2021. His own account to Billboard put his share at 50%, with Universal holding the rest. The claim covered more than he held.

"The Swift playbook transfers." Swift's re-records worked because her fans moved. Whether Ashanti's will is untested.

"A re-record takes the money." It takes the recording income on the new version. It leaves the song income where it was. The people credited on "Foolish" are paid either way. The originals also earn in ways a re-record does not reach. Aitch's 2022 single "Baby" sampled Ashanti's "Rock wit U" and reached No. 2 in the UK. Whoever controls the originals can license uses like that. (inference)

One more gap sits under all four. Her "20 years" has no public source except her. Newer label contracts reportedly run 10, 15 or 30 years. Whether a deal signed around 2001 carried a 20-year term is unknown.

What Game Is Being Played

This is Chartreuse's read, not a sourced claim. The game is a clock game. The label owns the asset past the end of the contract. The artist holds one lever, a contract clause with an expiry date. The statute offers a second chance in 2037, and the biggest label in the business is fighting it in court. The re-record turns the artist's permission into a competing asset, but only if the audience follows. Every party in the structure can wait. Only the artist's audience can drift.

What Happens Next

Two claims, tracked separately, since each breaks for a different reason.

Claim one: a released re-record takes real share from the original. This breaks if the album ships and the original's streams barely move, or if the new version's share stays low next to the original's.

Claim two: the statute stays closed to sound recordings long enough to matter. This breaks if the Second Circuit reverses the Salt-N-Pepa dismissal.

A third result changes the whole story. A filing or statement showing Ashanti already holds the original recordings would make the re-record a different kind of move.

What To Do With This

Audit your catalog before you need it. These steps reflect general music-industry practice. They are not specific to Black artists or to Ashanti's deals, and they are not legal advice.

  1. Inventory. For each valuable song, list the master owner and any co-owners, every credited writer and split, the publishers, the original agreement and every amendment.
  2. Read the re-record clause. Find its length, its start date and its scope. Get a lawyer to confirm the end date in writing before you book a studio.
  3. Clear the song layer. Co-writers and samples get paid on a new version too. "Foolish" carries five credited writers and one sample.
  4. Price the switch. Add up recording, clearance and marketing. Set the total against the revenue your audience can move. Test with one song before you record twelve.
  5. Own the new master in writing. Sign producer and musician agreements. Choose a distributor that does not take the rights you are recovering.
  6. Score it against the original. Track the new version's streams and income next to the old version's. Zero is the wrong baseline.

Run Ashanti's plan through step 6 today. There is nothing yet to score.

Glossary

Catalog. The full set of recordings and songs a person or company owns.

Chain of title. The paper trail of sales and transfers that shows who owns something now.

Cover license. A legal permission that lets anyone record a new version of a released song if they pay its writers a set fee.

Imprint. A label that works under the name and umbrella of a bigger company.

Master. The original recording of a song. Every copy comes from it.

℗ notice. The small line on a release that names who claims to own the recording.

Publishing. The rights to the song itself, meaning the words and the melody, apart from any recording of it.

Re-record restriction. A contract term that bars an artist from remaking old songs for a set number of years.

Stake. A share of ownership.

Termination right. A law that lets a creator take back a transfer of rights after 35 years.

Work made for hire. A work the law treats as made for an employer, so the employer counts as the author.
