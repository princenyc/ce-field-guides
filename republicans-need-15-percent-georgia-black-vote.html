Republicans Need 15% of Georgia's Black Vote. They've Never Gotten Past 11.

CE Field Guides · Published August 5, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a campaign talking point and ends with the number that talking point has to beat.

Republicans need roughly 15 percent of Georgia's Black vote to beat Jon Ossoff in November. That's the number Camilla Moore, head of the Georgia Black Republican Council, gave a Collins campaign audience in July. An AARP poll taken the same month has Ossoff ahead 52 to 43. The margin is nine points. The number Moore needs is one no Georgia Republican has ever delivered.

Why It Matters

Political campaigns treat Black voters as a demographic to court, not a bloc that sets terms. Moore's number changes the question. Not who owns the Black vote. What a small, organized fraction can demand when a race is close enough that it decides the outcome. The mechanism is real. Whether this campaign proves it is still open.

The Ceiling

Donald Trump won 11 percent of Georgia's Black vote in 2024. That was a record for a Georgia Republican, and it helped him carry the state. Moore is asking Collins to beat that record by more than a third. In a year when Georgia's Democratic primary turnout already outpaced Republican turnout by 15 points. In a year when federal litigation over Senate Bill 202 produced 2024 election data showing the law disproportionately burdened Black voters' access to the ballot. The ceiling isn't stalled at zero. It's real, and it sits below where Moore needs it.

The Scrutiny

Collins isn't making the case from a clean position. His son-in-law, David Alan Scheer II, has posted content from the white nationalist group Patriot Front, shared an antisemitic infographic he says Collins's daughter made, and told a podcast audience that white Americans need to "clear our land of other people." Scheer is registered to vote at a Collins-owned property. He's appeared in Collins's campaign photos and narrated ads for Collins's trucking company. When reporters pressed Collins on it in late July, he ended the press conference. Collins's former chief of staff was separately reported to have been in a group chat with Nick Fuentes and Richard Spencer. Most coverage of the son-in-law story traces back to a single CNN investigation. The facts hold up. The volume of outlets repeating them isn't extra corroboration.

No one has put a number on what the Georgia Black Republican Council gets in exchange for its work.

The Defection

Atlanta radio host Shelley Wynter, a Black conservative, has already defected to Ossoff. He said he doesn't want a senator who serves as a rubber stamp. Whether that's connected to the Scheer story isn't confirmed. It's the kind of thing that happens when a persuasion campaign runs into scrutiny it can't answer.

What This Is

No appointment, no procurement commitment, no policy trade between the GBRC and the Collins campaign is on record. If the leverage described here is real, it hasn't been cashed in yet. What's observable is a campaign courting a small, already-sympathetic Republican-aligned Black constituency for visibility and marginal turnout, while facing scrutiny that could suppress exactly the persuadable middle that strategy depends on.

What to Watch

AP VoteCast's Black-vote breakdown for the Georgia Senate race in November. Whether the GBRC secures anything in writing from Collins before Election Day. Further defections beyond Wynter. Whether Collins answers the questions he's so far declined to answer about Scheer. Early and absentee turnout composition data as it releases this fall. If November lands Collins closer to 11 percent than 15, the story was a media narrative. If it lands closer to 15, it's the first real proof this works.
