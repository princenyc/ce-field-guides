The Last Scrape: Africa's Fight to Own Its News

CE Field Guides · Published August 3, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a presidential directive and ends with the one legal distinction that actually matters.

Nigeria just moved a copyright fight into a courtroom built for something bigger. President Bola Tinubu has directed the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission to investigate Meta, Alphabet, X, and unnamed generative AI platforms operating in Nigeria. The complaint: anti-competitive practices and unauthorized use of Nigerian news content. The directive followed a joint petition from the Nigerian Press Organisation, the umbrella group covering the country's newspaper proprietors, journalists' union, editors' guild, broadcasters, and online publishers.

No wrongdoing has been established. The investigation is the event.

The Setup

Tinubu made this promise four months before he signed the directive. The petition gave him the occasion. The FCCPC's own statement names the scope: market dominance, unlawful exploitation of news content, and whether copyrighted articles and broadcast material were scraped or ingested to train AI models without permission.

This regulator has done this before. In 2025, the FCCPC won a $220 million judgment against Meta over data privacy violations. Meta is appealing. That case is the base rate for what happens next. Not a petition that dies in committee. A regulator that files, litigates, and collects.

The Game

Most AI-copyright fights run through copyright law. One work, one plaintiff, one infringement claim. Nigeria filed this as a competition case instead.

The difference matters. Copyright law asks whether a specific article was copied. Competition law asks whether a company's market position makes fair negotiation impossible in the first place. One case is about a text. The other is about a structure.

A copyright suit ends with a settlement on one library of content. A competition finding can end with a mandated payment structure across an entire market.

Platforms control discovery. Platforms control ad revenue splits. Platforms decide how much traffic a publisher gets back for the reporting it paid to produce. Publishers hold the facts. Platforms hold the door those facts have to pass through to reach anyone. Competition law lets a regulator subpoena the whole arrangement, not just the disputed page.

Who Pays, Who Watches

Meta, Alphabet, and X absorb legal cost now. Compliance cost later, if the FCCPC finds against them. A payment structure after that, if Nigeria follows the pattern already set by Australia's bargaining code and Canada's Online News Act, both cited by Nigerian outlets covering this story as the model Tinubu's government is watching.

The companies also have an exit ramp. Any FCCPC finding is reviewable in Nigerian federal court under Section 6(6)(b) of the constitution. That is where cases like this go to slow down, not where they go to die. Expect delay through litigation before expect a fine.

What to Watch

The petition stage tells you nothing. It is a letter. The Meta privacy case took years to produce a number. Watch for three things instead. A formal FCCPC finding or consent order, not another statement. Any Google News Showcase agreement signed in Nigeria during the investigation, since platforms cutting commercial deals mid-probe is how these fights usually end quietly, not with a ruling. A court challenge filed by any of the three companies under the constitutional review provision, since that is the platforms' actual playbook and it buys them time measured in years.

What Breaks the Thesis

This stops being a precedent if the FCCPC drops the matter without a public finding within roughly a year, or if it resolves through a private licensing deal instead of an enforcement action. A quiet settlement is not a competition-law precedent. It is a transaction, and transactions do not bind the next platform that walks into a smaller market and does the same thing.

What This Is

Not yet a story about what AI companies owe African publishers. A story about whether an African regulator can make that question stick. The FCCPC has one prior win on the board against one of the same companies. That is the only reason this is worth tracking instead of filing away as another petition.
