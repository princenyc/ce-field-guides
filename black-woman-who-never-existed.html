The Black Woman Who Never Existed

CE Field Guides · Published August 3, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with an Instagram post from a civil rights organization and ends with a legal system that has no real answer for what it's describing.

An AI account called Nia Noire pulled roughly one million TikTok followers and a video that hit 160 million views. Nia Noire has no face of her own. Color Of Change says it was built from someone else's, scraped from a real creator's videos without her permission or a check. The organization is calling it digital Blackface. The bigger story underneath that label is simpler and colder. AI just found a way to buy Black cultural labor without paying for it.

The Hook

On July 7, 2026, Color Of Change posted an Instagram video naming the practice directly. AI-generated Black women, built by copying the bodies, movements, and likenesses of real Black creators, running as monetized accounts across TikTok and Instagram. The organization is framing it as an evolution of digital Blackface, a term that used to mean reaction GIFs and memes. Now it means a synthetic person with a real audience, a real engagement rate, and real ad revenue attached.

The Pattern

The examples are specific. A 21-year-old creator from Sierra Leone named Josephine says her videos were used to build an AI persona with darker skin and exaggerated, sexualized features, without her knowledge. The Nia Noire account reportedly directs viewers to adult content. On TikTok, an operator running an account under the name Jezabel posts openly about the arrangement. Built by me, branded by me, monetized by me. No studio. No pretense. Just a person running a fake Black woman as a content business.

None of this is happening in a vacuum. Black influencers already earn roughly a third less than white influencers for comparable work, according to a 2024 industry survey. The AI version doesn't close that gap. It routes around the person entirely.

The transaction used to require hiring someone, however unfairly. AI removed even that requirement.

Who's Actually Getting Paid

Here's where the story gets less tidy than the framing suggests. There's no documented case of a named corporation running one of these accounts as a brand campaign. What's actually happening, as far as the record shows, is individual operators, mostly anonymous, monetizing through ad revenue share and affiliate links available to anyone on TikTok or Instagram. Corporate-backed AI brand ambassadors may be next. They aren't the current picture. The exploitation is real. The org chart is flatter and more anonymous than "Big Tech profits off Black women" implies.

The Model Itself

There's an older version of this story, and it points the wrong way. In 2019, researcher Eryk Salvaggio ran an early face-generation model, StyleGAN, and found it could barely render a convincing Black woman at all. Its training data was overwhelmingly white. A 2022 academic study of the same dataset put the number at 4 percent Black faces. That was framed as a bias problem: the machine hadn't seen enough Black faces to know what one looked like.

A 2025 benchmark of Veo3, the tool behind most of the current wave of AI influencers, found the same underrepresentation still holds. Left to its defaults, Veo3 skews toward Caucasian output, a pattern researchers attributed to its developer's Western-centric training data.

That means the accounts at the center of this story aren't an accident the model produces on its own. A general-purpose tool that defaults to white faces doesn't spontaneously generate a convincing, consistent version of one specific Black woman's look, over and over, at TikTok scale. Getting that requires deliberate work: prompting, fine-tuning, or feeding the model footage of a real person until it locks onto her specifically. The old bias story was about a machine that couldn't see Black women. This one is about operators making it see one Black woman in particular, closely enough to sell her.

The Legal Gap

Copyright doesn't cover a style. Right of publicity comes closer, but every case that's actually worked, the Lovo voice actors, the George Carlin estate, involved one identifiable person whose specific voice or likeness got copied. What's happening here is different. It's a composite drawn from many creators at once, which means no single person has clean legal standing to sue. Color Of Change's own policy arm flagged this gap back in December 2024, before this specific campaign existed, and proposed two fixes: a federal right of publicity law, since right now it's a patchwork of state statutes, and a royalty system that would pay creators whenever their style or likeness shows up in AI output. Neither exists yet.

One thing does already apply. The FTC's Endorsement Guides, updated in June 2023, explicitly cover virtual and AI-generated influencers. Any account taking brand money has to disclose it's AI-generated and disclose the material connection, same as a human creator would. That's a real, enforceable lever sitting unused.

What This Is

Not corporate strategy, not yet. An anonymous-operator business model that's cheaper than hiring a Black creator and legally cleaner than stealing one specific person's face. The style is the product. Nobody owns a style.

What to Watch

Whether the FTC brings its first enforcement action against an undisclosed AI-influencer brand deal. Whether a right-of-publicity suit succeeds against a composite persona rather than a single-identity impersonation, which would be the first sign the legal gap is actually closing. Progress, or lack of it, on Color Of Change's federal right-of-publicity and AI-royalty proposals. And whether the model shifts from anonymous individual operators to a named corporate account, which would confirm the trajectory the current framing assumes but the evidence doesn't yet show.
