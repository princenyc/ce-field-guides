The Contest the Trump Administration Won't Explain

CE Field Guides · Published July 29, 2026

What a Field Guide is: we strip the headline down to the incentive structure underneath it. Who built it, who gains, who pays, what happens next. This one starts with a disputed answer on a game show and ends with a contest built so that dispute could never be resolved.

A 15-year-old named Aangad Singh answered a question about the Thirteenth Amendment correctly and watched the screen mark it wrong. He said "Thirteenth." The auto-generated subtitles caught it. The judges didn't. Two other answers went the same way, both matching the Constitution's actual text. He finished fourth. The government running the contest wouldn't tell his family why.

That's the surface story. Underneath it is a machine built to never have to answer that question.

The Setup

The Presidential 1776 Award is a Department of Education competition, created to mark America's 250th birthday and run in partnership with the James Madison Memorial Fellowship Foundation, EBSCOed, and a coalition that includes the America First Policy Institute, Turning Point USA, and Hillsdale College. Over 8,000 students took an online exam in February. Regional semifinals cut that to 20. A nationally televised final at the Kennedy Center, hosted by Mario Lopez, cut 20 to 8, then 8 to 3. The top three got scholarships worth $250,000 combined and a trip to the Oval Office. CBS aired it. 1.63 million people watched, more than the daily average for CNN or MSNBC, a number the Department put in its own press release next to those two networks by name.

There was no live scoreboard. Contestants and their families kept their own tallies by hand. When Singh's mother asked the Department for the official scores after the broadcast, an official told her they were "proprietary."

What Actually Happened

Singh's father built a spreadsheet from the broadcast footage. His count: Michigan 43, Wyoming 42, Washington 41, Singh 40. One point short of third. Two of his marked-wrong answers, if scored the way the Constitution's own text reads, would have put him at 42, tied for the final spot.

The three who advanced were a homeschooler from Wyoming, a private-school student from Michigan, and a public-school student from Washington who'd told CNN before the contest that she supported Trump and later told the President, on camera, that she wanted to attend Hillsdale. He said he'd write her a recommendation.

Singh is Sikh, the son of Indian immigrants, and wears a turban. His mother and uncle believe that mattered. Singh and his father don't. On the record, in the reporting this story comes from, Singh says he thinks the judges just made a mistake, or worked from a rubric that didn't match his phrasing. That split inside one family is the most honest part of this story, and most of the coverage since has flattened it into something cleaner than it is.

The Game

Forget whether any one judge was biased. Nobody outside that room can prove it either way, and the family itself doesn't agree. The actual finding is simpler and more durable: this contest was built so that question could never be answered.

No live scoreboard. No published rubric. No appeal. The rules contestants agreed to say the judges' decision is "final and binding." When Singh's father asked for a formal review, the Department's response, three weeks later, didn't address a single one of his questions. It just cited the rule that says the decision is final.

That's not a bug in a civics contest. It's the design.

Who's Actually in the Room

The James Madison Memorial Fellowship Foundation wrote the questions and graded them, officially "independent" of the Department. That's true on paper. Nobody has shown what control the Department, the broadcast producers, or Freedom 250 organizers had over the rubric, the question selection, or what got cut for time. "Independent judges" is doing a lot of work in the Department's defense, and it's a claim nobody has actually tested.

Follow the money instead. Hillsdale College donated $10 million to the Freedom 250 committee that helped build this thing. EBSCOed, a commercial credentialing company, collected a Learning & Employment Record on every one of the 8,000-plus kids who entered, a permanent data asset built on the back of a scholarship contest. The America First Policy Institute and Turning Point USA are named coalition partners. None of that proves the fix was in. All of it explains who wanted this contest to exist, and who it was built to please.

What Happens If You Complain

Singh's father did the responsible thing. He emailed the Department asking for a review. He consulted lawyers about an administrative or civil rights path. Weeks passed. Nothing came back until a reporter's deadline forced a one-paragraph response that didn't engage with a single specific question.

That's not incompetence. The Department's Office for Civil Rights, the body that would normally hear a discrimination complaint like this, has had roughly 1,300 staff cut and lost entire regional office teams since this administration took over. Race-discrimination complaints have been deprioritized relative to other categories the office now prioritizes. If the Singh family files a formal complaint, the honest odds of it getting a real investigation, on any timeline that matters to a 15-year-old, are low. That's documented independently of this contest. It's not speculation.

What to Watch

The scorecards. A FOIA request for the actual judges' rubric from the June 9 final would settle more of this than anything else. Nobody has filed one yet, as far as the public record shows.

Whether a formal OCR complaint gets filed, and what happens to it. That's the more useful thing to track here, a live test of whether the civil rights complaint process still functions inside this Department at all.

Next year's contest. A live scoreboard costs nothing and fixes most of this. Watch whether the Department adds one, or runs it back exactly the same way.

What to Do With This

Don't file this under "rigged contest, racist judges." The evidence doesn't get you there, and the family closest to it doesn't agree on it. File it under something more useful: a federal government built a nationally televised meritocracy contest with no scoreboard, no rubric, and no appeal, funded in part by a college that got a public recommendation from the President for one of the winners. Whether or not this specific outcome was steered, the system was built so no one could ever prove it either way.
